## 1. Conclusions and Terminology <a href="#section-001" id="section-001"></a>

2026-09-13 addition: [graph memory, model equivalence, and computing foundations](../guide/mathematical-computing-foundation.md) applies this framework to SGD-Net/Transformer task and cost comparisons, then extends it to software and chip planning, distinguishing persistent graphs, compressed states, and parameter memory.

Evaluating models through function spaces, functional analysis, and numerical approximation is reasonable and closer to practical tasks. Architectures specify available representation sets, interaction mechanisms, inductive biases, and computational algorithms; these determine representational efficiency, learning difficulty, and numerical behavior for classes of tasks. Universal approximation only establishes the existence of sufficiently large approximators under certain conditions, not trainability with few samples or acceptable costs.

Distinguish functions f mapping inputs to outputs; functionals J mapping functions/trajectories to scalars, such as energy or task cost; and operators S that may map one function to another, such as “material-coefficient field → solution field.” Here, the “functional-space perspective” specifically means joint analysis of function spaces, operator mappings, constraint sets, and evaluation functionals.

Neural operators, DeepONet, and related research already provide foundations, so “understanding AI through function spaces” itself is not a new contribution. This project's candidate increment is using credible error evidence to select local representations, temporal memory, implicit computation, or new observations. [Neural operators](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/21-1524.html), [DeepONet](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-021-00302-5)

## 2. Express Every Task as a Mathematical Task Contract <a href="#section-002" id="section-002"></a>

Let inputs a∈X and outputs u∈Y. X/Y may be finite-dimensional spaces, L², Sobolev spaces, trajectory spaces, graph-signal spaces, or probability-distribution spaces. Conditions c include geometry, embodiment, experimental conditions, and boundaries. First define

$$
\mathcal K(a,c)=\{u\in Y:C_j(a,u,c)=0,\;G_k(a,u,c)\leq0\},
$$
$$
\mathcal S(a,c)=\{u\in\mathcal K(a,c):F(a,u,c)=0\},
$$

or define `argmin_{u∈K} J(a,u,c)` for suitable tasks. These constraint and solution sets generally are not linear spaces; calling them “solution spaces” does not imply unrestricted linear superposition. They may also be empty, have multiple solutions, or depend discontinuously on inputs.

Treat S as a single-valued solution operator only when a unique solution exists under declared conditions or an explicit solution branch is selected. Multiple-solution/stochastic tasks may learn conditional distributions or sets, rather than replacing all modes with one average solution; the mean may itself violate constraints.

| Contract field | Questions that must be answered |
|---|---|
| Inputs and observability | What are the true inputs? Which quantities do sensors/sampling provide? Which variables are unknown? |
| X/Y and regularity | Finite-dimensional quantities or functions? Smooth, piecewise smooth, discontinuous, or stochastic? |
| Constraints and solution selection | Which are exact laws and which approximate priors? Is the solution unique? |
| Symmetries and representations | How do permutations, Euclidean/manifold transformations, chirality, and periodic boundaries act? |
| Error metrics | Which matter: state values, derivatives, events, distributions, constraints, or final task costs? |
| Distributions and out-of-domain scope | Which inputs come from training distributions and which are new parameters/conditions? |
| Resources and evidence | How are sampling, labels, compute, latency, and storage charged? How reliable are reference solutions? |

When biological mechanisms are incompletely known, specify only evidence-supported measurement constraints and conditional probability targets, rather than inventing complete F or unified energy functions. Conditional associations in finite experimental data also do not automatically become intervention solution operators.

## 3. Fitting, Interpolation, Extrapolation, and Solving Are Four Different Problems <a href="#section-003" id="section-003"></a>

Fitting estimates functions from data and losses; interpolation requires passing through specified samples; extrapolation concerns uncovered regions or different conditions; numerical solving computes solutions of defined equations. These can be combined but cannot replace each other.

For example, sampling at x_j=j/m makes the zero function and sin(2πmx) identical at every sample point. Given only these samples, a model cannot determine which is the true function; adding leaves or hidden dimensions cannot automatically recover missing information. Additional regularity assumptions, frequency limits, or observations are needed.

Exact interpolation of noisy measurements may fit noise; low prediction error also does not imply accurate derivatives. Good fits to structural coordinates or energies do not automatically establish correct forces, stresses, or dynamics. For forces F=−∇E, evaluate appropriate derivative norms or actual force errors.

Interpolation capability is therefore only a representation test, not a standalone metric of intelligence, generalization, or scientific utility. Increasing training data, densifying sampling, expanding capacity, and extending solves are distinct operations.

## 4. Error Decomposition: Deciding What to Do Next <a href="#section-004" id="section-004"></a>

Let P_h:X→X_h be sampling/observation discretization, R_h:Y_h→Y bounded linear reconstruction, S_h a selected discrete reference mapping, and Mθ the learner. The deployed output is R_h\widetilde MθP_h. For a given input distribution and compatible norms, choosing reference parameters θ* in the same hypothesis class gives, by the triangle inequality,

$$
\|S-R_h\widetilde M_{\hat\theta}P_h\|
\leq \underbrace{\|S-R_hS_hP_h\|}_{\text{观测/离散表示误差}}
+\underbrace{\|R_h(S_h-M_{\theta^*})P_h\|}_{\text{表示逼近误差}}
$$
$$
\quad+\underbrace{\|R_h(M_{\theta^*}-M_{\hat\theta})P_h\|}_{\text{学习与优化误差}}
+\underbrace{\|R_h(M_{\hat\theta}-\widetilde M_{\hat\theta})P_h\|}_{\text{数值实现/求解误差}}.
$$

Factoring R_h outside differences above requires linearity; with nonlinear reconstruction, retain differences of reconstructed outputs, then estimate using Lipschitz conditions where needed. A norm such as L²(μ;Y) must make every term defined, finite, and in the same output space. θ* may be a best or near-best approximator; this inequality is an analytical decomposition, not a guarantee that all four terms are measurable online. Further separating statistical and optimization errors requires additional learning-theoretic assumptions; reference-model bias, noise, and distribution drift are not automatically separated by this formula either.

For stochastic systems or insufficiently observed inputs, replace S with an appropriate conditional-mean/distribution/risk target. Irreducible randomness and missing information must not be mistakenly attributed to model capacity.

| Evidence of dominant error | Candidate measures | Measures not adopted by default |
|---|---|---|
| Insufficient sampling, aliasing, or missing modalities | Add observations; change sampling/sensors and reconstruction | Indefinitely long inference |
| Insufficient approximation representations | Add local experts, graph resolution, feature order, or global coupling | Unconditionally add every module |
| Insufficient statistical generalization | Add reliable data, regularization, domain adaptation, or reduce capacity | Treat training errors as generalization evidence |
| Inadequate optimization | Improve training conditions, preconditioning, or initialization | Directly declare the architecture inexpressive |
| Solve/quantization errors | Adjust tolerances, precision, preconditioning, or solve methods | Claim physical correctness from small residuals |
| Prior/model mismatch | Revise equations, constraints, labels, or conditioning variables | Force projection into an incorrect feasible set |

Neural-operator/DeepONet error theory already has dedicated research; applicability conditions require individual checks and cannot directly replace SGD-Net analysis. [DeepONet error analysis](https://doi.org/10.1093/imatrm/tnac001)

## 5. Why Conditioning and Residual Reliability Must Be Evaluated <a href="#section-005" id="section-005"></a>

For A=diag(1,ε) and b=0, the true solution is 0. Candidate x=(0,1) has solution error 1 but residual ε; when ε is tiny, extremely small residuals still accompany large errors. The general claim “a posteriori residual reduction → proportional true-error reduction” is false.

A conditional positive example: in a linear weak problem, V is a Hilbert space, and a(·,·) is coercive, `a(v,v)≥α||v||²_V`, α>0; u and approximation u_h satisfy the same essential boundary conditions, error e∈V, and residual `r(v)=ℓ(v)−a(u_h,v)`. Then

$$
\alpha\|e\|_V^2\leq r(e)\leq\|r\|_{V^*}\|e\|_V,
\quad\|e\|_V\leq\|r\|_{V^*}/\alpha.
$$

This shows the importance of the residual's dual norm, stability constants, and boundary conditions. Residual MSE computed by a neural network at finite collocation points is not automatically `||r||_{V*}`; discretization, quadrature, and coverage analysis are also needed. Nonlinear, noncoercive, or multiple-solution problems require different stability/error theories.

F01 should distinguish “error estimators with upper-bound conditions,” “empirical error indicators,” and “unknown”; only then can F03 select growth or additional observations. Lyapunov conditions in 44 may constrain some dynamical errors, but do not universally replace boundary-value stability constants or inverse-problem identifiability.

## 6. Architecture Capabilities from Mathematical Representations <a href="#section-006" id="section-006"></a>

The following compares mechanisms, not a unified ranking; advantages depend on data, target classes, and budgets.

| Architecture/method | Main representation and priors | Commonly suited problems | Limitations to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| MLP/coordinate networks | Nonlinear compositional function families | Finite-dimensional regression, continuous coordinate fields | High frequencies/local singularities, derivative errors, training difficulty |
| CNN | Locally shared kernels and multiscale hierarchies | Local grid correlations, images/fields | Long-range coupling, irregular geometry, boundary handling |
| Transformer | Data-dependent globally weighted interactions | Nonlocal relationships, context selection | Sampling/position semantics, interaction costs, extrapolation |
| GNN/EGNN | Graph-local interactions and function subclasses under specified groups | Entity relationships, geometric physics, variable object counts | Aggregation expressivity, information bottlenecks, incorrect symmetry priors |
| SSM/recurrent models | Causal mappings from histories to states | Long sequences, fading memory, dynamic responses | Information capacity of finite states, stiffness/long-horizon errors |
| Splines/finite elements/kernel methods | Local bases, trial spaces, or RKHS regularization | Small-data smooth problems, known numerical structures | Dimension, kernel/grid choice, nonlocal interactions and complex geometry |
| DeepONet | Input-function encoding combined with output basis functions | Function-to-function solution mappings | Sensor points, low-rank structure, cross-discretization and error conditions |
| Fourier/graph neural operators | Operator families with learnable integral kernels/spectral representations | Parameterized PDEs, field prediction across sampling schemes | High frequencies/interfaces, quadrature, boundaries, resolution transfer |
| Trees/MoE | Local approximation and combination after partitioning | Heterogeneity, piecewise rules, differing operating regimes | Routing boundaries, continuity, overfitting sparse data |
| SGD-Net | Combined graph–temporal–local-expert and adaptive-solve classes | Mixed structures, local complexity, and tasks with available feedback | Component errors and coupling costs; no inherent superiority to all methods above |

If a solution set is approximable by few modes, low-rank/basis expansions may be efficient; moving interfaces, contact switching, or complex multimodal solutions may require local partitions or nonlinear representations. Measure growth in required basis count/capacity as target error decreases; approximation widths of linear subspaces are not directly lower bounds for arbitrary nonlinear networks.

Comparisons should plot error against parameters/effective degrees of freedom, training samples, inference time, and memory. Parameter counts alone are unfair: methods differ in reference computations, graph construction, iterations, context, and data-pretraining costs.

## 7. Interpret SGD Growth as Adaptive Approximation Without Claiming Finite-Element Theorems <a href="#section-007" id="section-007"></a>

One research representation template is

$$
\hat u(a,x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_\ell(a,x)\,u_{\theta_\ell}(a,x),
\quad\pi_\ell\geq0,\quad\sum_\ell\pi_\ell=1.
$$

It describes soft combinations of local experts; not every SGD instance must use this form. Hard routing may be discontinuous at boundaries, and soft combinations do not guarantee conservation or constraint satisfaction. Node/expert counts are not the only effective degrees of freedom.

- **h-type refinement:** Refine computational grids, regions, or representation nodes with P/Q state migration. This does not add real atoms or fingers.
- **p-type enhancement:** Increase local basis order, feature/state capacity, or richness of local operators. Neural-network widening is only an analogy, not finite-element polynomial-order elevation.
- **Expert/dictionary expansion:** Add local models for different physical modes or conditions, deciding retention from independent posterior outcomes.
- **Solve refinement:** Add iterations, change preconditioning or precision, and improve solving within a fixed representation; this need not increase representational capability.
- **Data refinement:** Add observations or reliable reference labels to address missing information; this is not adding neural layers.

Classical Galerkin methods also involve weak forms, trial/test spaces, and stability conditions. Without these definitions, graph messages cannot be called rigorous Galerkin discretizations or inherit their convergence orders. Where applicable equations exist, a concrete Galerkin/hybrid submodule can be designed and validated.

## 8. Five Internal Interfaces That Actually Need Completing <a href="#section-008" id="section-008"></a>

No top-level unit counts are added; the following belong to existing parent units.

| Internal interface | Parent units | Explicit definition |
|---|---|---|
| FN01 Task-space contract | A01/A02/A06, L01 | X/Y, measurement operators, constraints, regularity, norms, well-posedness, and objectives |
| FN02 Sampling and reconstruction | A07, B08, C04/C06, E08 | P_h/R_h, coordinates, cell volumes/quadrature weights, cross-resolution mappings |
| FN03 Operator/query readout | D02/D03, E01/E05/E08 | Outputs fields/trajectories given function observations and query locations; add local or global kernels by task |
| FN04 Residual quality and stability factors | F01/F02, L03/L04, IM04 | Residual norms, empirical/reliable evidence levels, sources of condition numbers or stability constants |
| FN05 Error–cost adaptation | F03–F07, EV02–EV08, G07 | Map candidate actions to expected error reduction/cost and accept only after validation |

A message form suitable for field tasks is

$$
v'_i=\sigma\left(Wv_i+\sum_j w_j\kappa_\theta(x_i,x_j,a_i,a_j)v_j\right),
$$

where w_j are quadrature/volume weights consistent with discrete sampling. This explicitly connects graph messages with integral operators, but parameter sharing or added weights alone do not prove mesh independence; kernel regularity, quadrature consistency, graph support domains, and cross-resolution error validation are also needed. Arbitrary graph mean aggregation should not automatically be interpreted as physical integration. The JMLR neural-operator work is a direct precedent for such designs; this form is not claimed as the project's invention.

The functional interpretation thus becomes: GNN/EGNN selects spatial interactions and symmetric function subclasses; SSM selects history compression; trees select local representations; implicit layers select equation-solving methods; Lyapunov/numerical conditions check propagation of specified errors; the posterior feedback loop selects the next investment in computation or observations.

## 9. Domain Correspondence and Theoretical Objectives <a href="#section-009" id="section-009"></a>

World models evaluate conditional trajectories/distributions, prediction horizons, and closed-loop losses; physical AI evaluates tracking, contact events, and feasibility; molecules/materials separate structures, energies, derivatives, properties, and processing endpoints; cell/neural/signaling tasks concern populations or real time series, conditional changes, measurement noise, and held-out-subject generalization. There is no need to force every task into a PDE.

Reasonable initial theoretical questions include approximation error under fixed domains and components; stability of P/Q in specific norms; whether defined residuals bound target errors; whether local refinement improves error–cost tradeoffs; controllability of temporal/graph-switching errors; and when missing observations prevent identification. Each question targets explicit configurations rather than proving the whole 72-unit system at once.

Research conclusions should center on errors and costs over task solution sets. Mathematical expressivity of components is foundational, but judging whether problems can be solved requires combining it with observation information, constraint suitability, learning, and numerical stability.
